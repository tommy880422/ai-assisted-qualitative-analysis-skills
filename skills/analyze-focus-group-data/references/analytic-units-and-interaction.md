# Analytic Units and Interaction

## Declare the Unit

Focus-group analysis can use several nested units:

| Unit | What it can show | Risk if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Speaking turn or excerpt | A localized claim or formulation | Decontextualization |
| Participant | Variation in position or trajectory | Treating group data as individual interviews |
| Interactional episode | How meaning develops through exchange | Missing broader group pattern |
| Subgroup or alliance | Coalitions, status, and patterned difference | Reifying temporary alignment |
| Whole group | Shared language, norms, atmosphere, and negotiated positions | Erasing minority voices |
| Cross-group comparison | Contextual or category-based variation | Pseudo-quantitative replication |

Use more than one layer when the research question concerns both substantive perspectives and group interaction.

## Match the Transcript to the Declared Unit

The transcript sets a ceiling on what the analysis can claim. Check that ceiling before promising interaction analysis.

- A full transcript is not mandatory. Time-pressured or narrowly applied questions can be answered from detailed observer notes and replays of the recording. Decide this in advance rather than discovering later that the record cannot support the claim.
- Speaker attribution has to be designed into the session. Name cards, a seating chart, or a round of self-identification give the transcriber a reference point; without one, turns cannot be reliably assigned and every participant-level or subgroup-level claim collapses.
- Editing is a trade-off. Transcripts faithfully record incomplete sentences, half-finished thoughts, and odd phrasing. Light editing improves readability, but heavy cleaning is counterproductive when the point is how participants think and talk. Preserve the character of contributions even when the grammar is poor: confusion about a concept can itself be a finding.
- Do not let the moderator fill gaps from memory. Recall is fallible, and knowing how the discussion ended colours what the moderator believes was said earlier. Mark reconstructed material as reconstructed.
- A transcript omits nonverbal communication, gesture, and behavioural response, and it flattens tone. "That is bad" can mean approval or condemnation. Anything the analysis rests on that the transcript cannot carry must come from observer notes, systematic event recording, or video review, and those exist only if their collection was planned before fieldwork.
- Record the transcription rules used, who transcribed, whether software was involved, and any cleaning or translation, so the claim ceiling is visible to readers.

## Interaction Code Families

Add interaction codes selectively:

- initiates a position;
- agrees, echoes, amplifies, or completes;
- qualifies or limits;
- challenges, contradicts, or reframes;
- offers example or counterexample;
- invokes experience, identity, expertise, policy, or common sense;
- builds consensus or closes discussion;
- uses humor, irony, hesitation, or repair;
- shifts topic or avoids response;
- invites, interrupts, overlaps, or excludes;
- changes position;
- responds to a stimulus or moderator intervention;
- produces collective language, story, ranking, or solution.

Do not code every conversational feature unless the question requires conversation-level analysis.

## Read Context Around Every Extract

For candidate evidence, capture:

1. the prompting question or event;
2. at least the preceding and following turns needed to understand meaning;
3. who introduced and who took up the position;
4. agreement, resistance, laughter, pause, or nonresponse;
5. moderator intervention;
6. whether the position recurred or changed later;
7. group composition and setting factors relevant to interpretation.

## Interpret Silence and Absence Carefully

Differentiate:

- topic never introduced;
- question asked but no one answered;
- one position asserted without challenge;
- visible or audible hesitation;
- participant withdrew after interruption;
- issue appeared in some groups but not others;
- transcript or technology failed to capture the event.

Absence can be analytically important, but it requires a documented comparison and cannot carry a single fixed meaning.
