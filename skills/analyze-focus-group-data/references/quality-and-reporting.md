# Quality and Reporting

## Quality Audit

Check:

- alignment between group design and analytic comparisons;
- complete identification of groups, speakers, and field materials;
- transcript adequacy for the level of interaction claimed;
- explicit analytic units;
- documented coding, memoing, and matrix decisions;
- analysis of group interaction and moderator influence;
- attention to negative cases, absences, and rival explanations;
- reflexivity about researcher, moderator, culture, and power;
- separation of data, interpretation, and recommendation;
- sufficient contextual detail for transfer judgments;
- ethical handling of recognizable group stories and quotations.

## Journal Reporting Checklists

COREQ names interview and focus-group studies as its explicit scope, so a focus-group manuscript submitted to a health or nursing journal will almost certainly need it. Its 32 items cover the research team and reflexivity, the study design, and the analysis and findings — which maps onto the reporting structure below with two recurring friction points.

- **Items written for individual interviews.** Several assume one participant per encounter. Where an item asks about participant numbers or data saturation, answer at the level your design actually used — the group, and the planned comparison across groups — and say so, rather than converting group data into a participant count to fill the cell.
- **Items that assume a coding-reliability stance.** If your analysis treats interpretation as situated, report what you did and note that the item's premise does not hold for this design.

Complete the checklist; describe deviations in one sentence each. A reasoned deviation is defensible, a blank is not, and a fabricated compliance claim is worse than either.

## Reporting Structure

1. **Design:** why focus groups and why these group comparisons.
2. **Participants and groups:** composition and recruitment without false representativeness.
3. **Data generation:** questions, stimuli, setting, mode, moderators, recording, and deviations.
4. **Analysis:** unit, coding or interpretive procedure, within- and cross-group comparison, interaction treatment, and reflexivity.
5. **Findings:** organize around analytic claims; show interaction, variation, and counter-evidence.
6. **Discussion:** connect findings to theory, context, implications, and alternative explanations.
7. **Limitations:** design-specific constraints rather than generic disclaimers.

## Quotation Practice

- Identify the group and speaker using nonidentifying labels.
- Include multiple turns when interaction is part of the evidence.
- Explain the prompt and relevant context.
- Avoid stitching speakers into a false continuous conversation.
- Do not use one vivid quotation to stand for all groups.
- Balance readability with enough turn-taking detail to support the claim.
- Reassess deductive disclosure when participants know one another.

## Recommendations

Link every recommendation to:

- a stated finding;
- the groups and contexts that produced it;
- the mechanism or concern inferred;
- uncertainty and possible unintended effects;
- who can act and what evidence would monitor implementation.

Do not convert participant preferences directly into universal recommendations.
