## 1. Design logic

Build a visible chain:

> research purpose → research question → JTBD formulation → construct definitions → unit of analysis → sampling contrasts → data source → analysis → warranted claim

Repair any link that does not follow. For example, do not ask a mechanism question, collect only feature-preference ratings, and then claim to explain switching.

Use JTBD in one of four roles:

- **Sensitizing lens:** Let JTBD direct attention while allowing inductive concepts to emerge.
- **Analytic framework:** Define constructs before coding and use them in framework analysis or qualitative content analysis.
- **Explanatory proposition:** Test a specified mechanism, such as how push/pull/habit/anxiety interact in switching.
- **Applied innovation process:** Translate insights into job maps, desired outcomes, or solution criteria.

Name the role. Do not claim a confirmatory theory test when the design uses JTBD only as a loose interview prompt.

## 2. Research-question patterns

### 2.1 Descriptive

- What progress are [actors] trying to make when [circumstance]?
- Which functional, emotional, and social dimensions characterize this job?
- Which current approaches and workarounds are used to get the job done?

Suitable evidence: rich episode interviews, observation, diaries, documents.

### 2.2 Process or mechanism

- How do push, pull, habit/inertia, and anxiety shape the transition from [current approach] to [new approach]?
- How does the job change across the trigger, search, choice, first-use, and repeated-use stages?
- Under what circumstances does a Big Hire fail to become a Little Hire?

Suitable evidence: switching timelines, multiple temporal perspectives, use traces, follow-up interviews.

### 2.3 Comparative

- How do job circumstances and success criteria differ between adopters and non-adopters?
- How do the jobs of service users, frontline staff, managers, and funders align or conflict?
- How does the same apparent job differ across institutional settings?

Suitable evidence: purposive contrast sampling and case-by-variable matrices.

### 2.4 Opportunity or design

- Which job steps and desired outcomes are most poorly served by current approaches?
- What obstacles to adoption and use should an intervention address?
- Which underserved outcomes define distinct opportunity segments?

Suitable evidence: qualitative construct generation; optional quantitative rating and segmentation; feasibility data.

### 2.5 Implementation

- What jobs must each stakeholder accomplish for [intervention] to be adopted, delivered, and sustained?
- Which implementation barriers function as switching anxieties, institutional constraints, or conflicting stakeholder jobs?
- How should an intervention be adapted without weakening its core function?

Suitable evidence: multi-stakeholder interviews, observations, workflow documents, iteration logs, implementation outcomes.

### 2.6 Questions JTBD cannot answer alone

Add another design when asking:

- Does the intervention cause an outcome?
- How prevalent is a job in a population?
- Which solution is clinically safest or most effective?
- Is an innovation disruptive at an industry level?
- How do power, inequality, discourse, or institutions produce preferences?

Use JTBD as one layer, then add experimental, epidemiological, survey, institutional, critical, or longitudinal analysis as appropriate.

## 3. Unit of analysis and case boundaries

Default to a **job episode**: a bounded sequence in which an actor encounters a circumstance, seeks progress, considers or uses solutions, and experiences consequences.

Record:

- case ID and participant/pseudonym ID;
- episode start and end;
- focal actor and role;
- setting and circumstance;
- trigger or struggling moment;
- prior/current solution;
- alternatives considered;
- decision or non-decision;
- first use and repeated use if relevant;
- perceived result;
- evidence sources.

Allow more than one episode per participant. Avoid treating every participant as one homogeneous case when they describe distinct jobs in different circumstances.

For organizational or health systems, use an embedded case design:

- system/organization as the outer case;
- stakeholder role as an embedded unit;
- job episode as the primary analytic event.

State which level each claim concerns.

## 4. Sampling and recruitment

> Use `plan-qualitative-sampling` for the sampling strategy, adequacy reasoning, recruitment of hard-to-reach participants, and the stopping rule. This section adds only what is JTBD-specific: which contrasts are theoretically consequential, and that eligibility is defined on an episode rather than a person.

### 4.1 Build a sampling frame from theory-relevant contrasts

Consider:

- switchers versus non-switchers;
- recent adopters versus long-term users;
- successful use versus abandonment;
- consumers versus nonconsumers;
- ordinary use versus workaround or unusual use;
- low- versus high-constraint circumstances;
- buyer, executor, supporter, gatekeeper, implementer, and affected party;
- different sites, policies, resource levels, and cultural contexts;
- positive, negative, and deviant cases.

Do not recruit only loyal current users. They hide firing decisions, nonconsumption, and unmet jobs.

### 4.2 Use criterion-based purposive sampling

Define an eligible episode, not just an eligible person. Example criteria:

- experienced the focal decision within a recall window;
- personally participated in the decision or use;
- can discuss one concrete episode;
- belongs to a specified stakeholder role;
- covers a needed analytic contrast.

Keep demographic variables only when theoretically or ethically relevant; do not assume they constitute job segments.

### 4.3 Plan sample adequacy

Justify adequacy using:

- specificity of the sample;
- depth and quality of episode evidence;
- complexity and heterogeneity of the phenomenon;
- number of stakeholder groups and comparison cells;
- analytic strategy;
- availability of triangulating data;
- recurrence and variation of candidate job mechanisms;
- ability to examine negative cases.

Avoid universal numeric rules. If proposing an initial target, label it provisional and attach a stopping review based on information adequacy. For multi-stakeholder research, plan enough cases within each consequential role to support within-role analysis before cross-role synthesis.

### 4.4 Recruit ethically

Avoid manager-controlled recruitment when participation could affect employment or care. Separate clinical/service provision from research consent. Provide accessible language and compensation that does not create undue influence. Plan alternatives for people with limited technology, literacy, mobility, or privacy.

## 5. Data-generation choices

| Evidence need | Preferred method | Main strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Switching decision | Critical-incident/switching interview | Temporal and motivational detail | Recall and post-hoc rationalization |
| Routine/tacit work | Observation or contextual inquiry | Behavior, workaround, context | Reactivity and privacy |
| Change over days/weeks | Diary plus follow-up | Near-time evidence | Burden and attrition |
| Shared norms/disagreement | Focus group | Interaction data | Conformity and lost episode depth |
| Multi-actor implementation | Role-specific interviews plus workflow observation | Stakeholder conflict and coordination | Role silos |
| Importance/satisfaction | Survey after qualitative development | Prioritization and distribution | Premature or unstable constructs |
| Actual use | Logs/records plus interview | Big-Hire/Little-Hire triangulation | Motive cannot be inferred from logs alone |
| Institutional constraint | Policy/workflow documents plus interviews | Formal versus enacted process | Documents may not reflect practice |

Combine methods only when each contributes a distinct form of evidence.

## 6. Mixed-method and ODI designs

### 6.1 Sequential exploratory design

1. Define the executor and provisional job.
2. Conduct qualitative job-map and outcome interviews.
3. Refine and de-duplicate outcome statements.
4. Conduct cognitive testing for comprehension and scale use.
5. Specify sampling frame and field a quantitative survey.
6. Analyze importance, satisfaction, opportunity scores, and optional segments.
7. Return to qualitative cases to explain surprising or divergent quantitative patterns.

Use this design for prioritization. Do not use ODI ratings collected from a convenience sample to claim population segments.

### 6.2 Embedded qualitative design

Use JTBD interviews within an intervention, implementation, or service study to explain uptake and use. Keep primary outcome evaluation separate from JTBD mechanism analysis.

### 6.3 Longitudinal design

Interview at decision, early use, and later use. Compare anticipated job, experienced job, and repeated-use evidence. This directly addresses Big-Hire/Little-Hire divergence and changing circumstances.

### 6.4 Quantitative reporting requirements

Report:

- target population and sampling frame;
- inclusion and exclusion;
- response and completion rates;
- item-development path;
- scale and coding transformation;
- missing-data treatment;
- sample size and rationale;
- reliability/measurement evidence appropriate to the instrument;
- score formula and threshold;
- uncertainty and sensitivity;
- segmentation variables, algorithm, model selection, stability, and validation;
- deviations from plan.

## 7. Ethics and data management

Plan:

- informed consent and withdrawal;
- whether recordings are optional;
- pseudonymization and removal of indirect identifiers;
- storage location, encryption, access, retention, and destruction;
- handling of distress, illegal activity, clinical risk, or workplace retaliation;
- translation and interpreter confidentiality;
- AI/transcription vendor disclosure and approval;
- whether quotations can identify a rare role or incident;
- linkage of interview, observation, survey, and usage data;
- member feedback without transferring interpretive authority or exposing other cases.

For health research, do not collect identifiable clinical detail unless necessary and approved. Do not let a JTBD interview substitute for clinical assessment or care.

## 9. Feasibility and quality audit

Before collection, verify:

- At least one question requires JTBD rather than generic user research.
- The selected formulation can answer the research question.
- Eligible participants can recall or be observed in a concrete episode.
- The sample includes theoretically useful contrasts.
- Each stakeholder group has a distinct analytic purpose.
- The interview can be completed without teaching JTBD to participants.
- The protocol separates current reality from proposed-solution reactions.
- The analysis can retain case context rather than fragmenting all transcripts.
- The consent and data plan covers sensitive episode details.
- The timetable includes transcription, memoing, negative-case checks, and revision.
- Any quantitative phase follows qualitative construct stabilization.
- The intended claim matches the evidence and sample.
