## 1. Safety and scope

Use JTBD to study choice, access, workflow, adoption, engagement, adherence, implementation, and service experience. Do not use it to determine diagnosis, clinical indication, treatment efficacy, or safety.

Require clinical, ethical, regulatory, and implementation evidence in addition to customer desirability. A job insight cannot override standards of care, informed consent, accessibility, safeguarding, or equity.

Avoid framing nonadherence as an individual failure. Examine treatment burden, financial pressure, access, cognition, symptoms, trust, caregiving, transportation, digital access, stigma, and institutional design.

## 2. Multi-stakeholder job architecture

Map roles separately. A typical system may include:

- patient/service user;
- family member or unpaid caregiver;
- peer supporter;
- clinician/prescriber;
- nurse/case manager/community worker;
- scheduler/front desk/administrator;
- clinic or service manager;
- payer/funder/procurement decision maker;
- IT, data, privacy, and support staff;
- regulator or policy actor;
- implementation team/researcher.

For every role, identify:

- circumstance and accountability;
- desired progress/job;
- practical, emotional, professional, social, financial, and ethical criteria;
- decision rights and constraints;
- current workflow, workaround, and non-action;
- push, pull, habit/inertia, and anxiety;
- adoption versus routine-use conditions;
- value received and cost/risk borne;
- dependencies and handoffs.

Do not use a single “healthcare customer” label. The executor, beneficiary, buyer, user, and accountable decision maker are often different.

## 3. Healthcare-specific circumstances

Probe only what is relevant and ethically approved:

- acuity, symptom variability, cognitive load, fatigue, pain, and comorbidity;
- timing relative to diagnosis, discharge, crisis, recovery, or routine care;
- health literacy, language, disability, and accessibility;
- trust, stigma, identity, privacy, and fear of judgment;
- cost, insurance, reimbursement, and time away from work/caregiving;
- transportation, food, housing, device, connectivity, and digital skills;
- continuity, fragmentation, wait times, and handoffs;
- professional scope, workload, supervision, and liability;
- documentation, interoperability, data governance, and cybersecurity;
- local policy, procurement, staffing, and incentives.

Treat these as potential circumstances, constraints, or rival explanations—not automatic codes in every case.

## 4. Implementation workflow

### Step 1: Define the implementation question

Specify whether the study examines reach, adoption, feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, fidelity, adaptation, implementation cost, penetration, or sustainability. JTBD can help explain stakeholder mechanisms but does not replace measurement of these outcomes.

### Step 2: State stakeholder hypotheses

For each role, state:

- proposed job;
- current approach;
- critical success criteria;
- predicted adoption/use forces;
- evidence that would disconfirm the hypothesis.

### Step 3: Sample contrasts

Include settings or roles with different workload, resources, authority, and experience. Include people who declined, discontinued, could not access, or informally adapted the intervention.

### Step 4: Reconstruct real cases

Ask each participant to walk through a recent episode. Compare formal workflow documents with enacted work. Observe handoffs and compensating behaviors where possible.

### Step 5: Analyze cross-role fit

Identify:

- where one role's solution creates work for another;
- where decision authority and implementation burden are separated;
- where patient value conflicts with reimbursement or throughput;
- where professional identity or perceived liability creates anxiety;
- where first adoption succeeds but routine delivery fails;
- where a local workaround preserves the core function;
- where adaptation removes a necessary active ingredient.

### Step 6: Connect to implementation outcomes

Form testable explanations, for example:

> When [circumstance], [role] is trying to [progress]. [Implementation strategy] may reduce [specific anxiety/barrier] and support [implementation outcome].

Test this with implementation and outcome data rather than presenting it as proven.

## 5. I-Corps integration

The supplied mHealth study illustrates one possible combination: stakeholder hypotheses, customer-discovery interviews, and qualitative coding informed adaptation of an SMS-supported obesity intervention in community mental-health settings. It sampled six stakeholder groups nested within three categories (service consumers, clinician consumers, decision makers), conducted 29 interviews, and reported both shared and role-specific themes. Treat this as an empirical example, not a universal protocol.

Use an I-Corps-style cycle:

1. Define stakeholder and value-proposition hypotheses.
2. Specify disconfirming evidence.
3. Interview or observe real stakeholders in concrete episodes.
4. Log expected versus observed findings.
5. Revise the job, barrier, stakeholder, or value hypothesis.
6. Decide whether to iterate, pivot, stop, or test again.
7. Preserve the hypothesis history.

Keep JTBD and I-Corps outputs distinct:

| JTBD output | I-Corps output |
|---|---|
| Job/circumstance | Testable customer/problem hypothesis |
| Alternatives and forces | Evidence from customer discovery |
| Success criteria/outcomes | Value-proposition hypothesis |
| Job statement/job map | Iteration or pivot decision |

Avoid sales pitching during discovery interviews. Do not count surveys as equivalent to open-ended interviews when depth and follow-up are required.

## 6. Intervention adaptation

Separate:

- **Core function:** mechanism or purpose that must be preserved.
- **Form:** delivery channel, wording, timing, staffing, and workflow that may be adapted.
- **Context:** local constraints and resources.
- **Implementation strategy:** training, facilitation, reminders, supervision, incentives, technical support, or policy change.

For each proposed adaptation, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Evidence | Which episodes and roles support it? |
| Job/criterion | Which progress or success criterion does it address? |
| Mechanism | How should it reduce a barrier or improve use? |
| Core-function risk | Could it weaken efficacy or fidelity? |
| Burden shift | Who gains work, cost, or risk? |
| Equity effect | Who may become more or less able to participate? |
| Safety/privacy | What new risk appears? |
| Test | What feasibility, clinical, or implementation evidence is needed? |
| Decision | Preserve, adapt, test, or reject |

Do not recommend an MVP for clinical deployment without safety, ethics, and regulatory review. A prototype suitable for research discussion may not be suitable for patient care.

### 7.1 Stakeholder job matrix

Rows: roles. Columns: circumstance, job, success criteria, current approach, forces, authority, burden, value, handoff, evidence, conflict.

### 7.2 Adoption-to-sustainment timeline

Track awareness, evaluation, approval, onboarding, first delivery/use, repeated delivery/use, adaptation, maintenance, and discontinuation. Mark Big Hire and Little Hire separately.

### 7.3 Handoff map

For each handoff, record sender, receiver, information/resource, success criterion, failure mode, workaround, and downstream consequence.

### 7.4 Equity matrix

Compare circumstances and barriers across access, language, disability, income, geography, caregiving, digital connectivity, stigma, and institutional trust where relevant. Do not use demographic categories as job definitions; use them to examine unequal circumstances and consequences.

## 8. Reporting and limitations

Report:

- stakeholder groups and who was not represented;
- site and policy context;
- how JTBD constructs and I-Corps activities were operationalized;
- interview count by role and episode type;
- analytic method beyond the JTBD label;
- adaptations linked to evidence;
- implementation and clinical outcomes kept separate;
- dissent, nonadoption, abandonment, and unintended effects;
- privacy, burden, safety, and equity implications;
- transfer conditions to other settings.

Avoid:

- inferring clinical benefit from stakeholder enthusiasm;
- treating decision-maker approval as patient adoption;
- treating enrollment as repeated use;
- assuming all barriers can be solved by product design;
- generalizing one site's job system to all health systems;
- using commercial case claims as clinical effectiveness evidence.
