## 1. How to use this ledger

This ledger covers the 14 PDFs supplied in the user's `JTBD` folder, rooted at `SOURCE_ROOT = <the folder on this computer that holds the JTBD sources — set this before first use>`. “PDF pages” refer to the file's page order, which may differ from printed pagination. Verify all quotations and final page citations against the original PDF.

**If `SOURCE_ROOT` has not been set to a real folder, or a listed file is missing:** work from this skill's reference files, tell the user that the book could not be opened, and do not give page numbers or quotations from it. The filenames below are suggestions; if the user's copy has a different name, match it by title and author. Page ranges were recorded from one particular copy and may differ in another edition or file — confirm against the user's copy before citing.


Use the ledger to locate evidence, not as a substitute for reading the relevant page. Paraphrase copyrighted works and quote only short passages needed for analysis.

Source classes:

- **Core conceptual:** defines or substantially develops JTBD.
- **Practitioner method:** provides applied procedures or tools.
- **Empirical application:** reports a study using JTBD or adjacent customer discovery.
- **Adjacent theory/method:** useful context but not itself JTBD.
- **Secondary synthesis:** summarizes other sources.

## 3. Detailed source notes

### 3.1 Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan — *Competing Against Luck*

**File:** `Christensen-2016-Competing against luck.pdf`

**Use for:**

- theory as a causal lens rather than correlation alone (PDF 24–27);
- definition of job as desired progress in a particular circumstance (PDF 28–30);
- distinction from generic needs, product attributes, customer traits, trends, and competition (PDF 28–31);
- functional, emotional, and social dimensions (PDF 28–31);
- job hunting through own experience, nonconsumption, workarounds, compensating behavior, and unusual use (chapter 4; summary PDF 67);
- Big Hire and Little Hire, firing old solutions, four switching forces, storyboard, and depth interviewing (PDF 68–83);
- job spec and the experience required to deliver progress (PDF 84–100);
- integration around the job, measurement, and organizational implications (PDF 102–139).

**Do not use for:** independent prevalence estimates or proof that one interview reveals a market-wide job. The book itself emphasizes clustering insight rather than relying on one interview.

### 3.2 Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan — HBR article

**File:** `Christensen-2016-Know your customers' jobs to be done.pdf`

**Use for:** a concise primary statement of the progress/circumstance lens, social-emotional-functional dimensions, job spec, struggling moments/forces, and a worked organizational example (PDF 4–10).

**Relationship:** The article and *Competing Against Luck* share authors, publication year, examples, and constructs. Cite both only when each contributes something distinct.

### 3.3 Christensen, Anthony, Berstell, and Nitterhouse — “Finding the Right Job for Your Product”

**File:** `Christensen-2007-Finding the right job for your product.pdf`

**Use for:**

- job-based market segmentation versus demographic/product categories;
- milkshake illustration and situation-sensitive choice;
- methods for identifying job structures;
- how job understanding shapes product, distribution, advertising, brand, and business plans;
- early academic-management articulation of JTBD in MIT Sloan Management Review.

**Evidence anchors:** PDF 1–3 for framing and definition; later pages for identification and business-plan implications; PDF 10 for references and source metadata.

### 3.4 Anthony and Sinfield — “Product for Hire”

**File:** `Anthony-2007-Product for hire.pdf`

**Use for:** job thinking across demand identification, solution optimization, defending share, capturing value, and revitalizing growth; investigative-reporter stance; sample interview questions (PDF 2–7).

**Do not use for:** a detailed fieldwork protocol. The article is a concise practitioner overview.

### 3.5 Bettencourt — “Debunking Myths About Customer Needs”

**File:** `Bettencourt-2009-Debunking myths about customer needs.pdf`

**Use for:** differentiating customer needs from solution ideas; arguing that customers can discuss needs/outcomes even when they cannot design technology; solution-neutral, outcome-oriented need statements (PDF 1–7).

**Critical note:** This source takes a stronger view of articulable customer metrics than Christensen's warning that customers may not accurately or completely articulate requirements. Preserve this productive tension. Resolve it methodologically by eliciting concrete episodes and metrics, then triangulating words with behavior.

### 3.6 Anthony, Johnson, Sinfield, and Altman — “Identifying Jobs to Be Done”

**File:** `Anthony-2008-Identifying jobs to be done.pdf`

**Use for:**

- five questions covering fundamental problem, evaluation objectives, barriers, available solutions, and solution quality (PDF 7–12);
- brainstorming and light interaction (PDF 14–16);
- interviews and stakeholder breadth (PDF 16–17);
- observation and diaries (PDF 17–18);
- compensating behaviors (PDF 19);
- customer case research and purchase-story interviewing (PDF 19–20);
- investigative-reporter and problem-centered question principles (PDF 20);
- exploratory prioritization by importance, frequency, and frustration (PDF 21–22);
- tool summaries and implementation reminders (PDF 31–33).

**Scoring distinction:** The chapter's exploratory score is `(importance + frequency) × frustration` on specified 1–5 inputs. It is not ODI's opportunity formula.

### 3.7 Wunker, Wattman, and Farber — *Jobs to Be Done: A Roadmap for Customer-Centered Innovation*

**File:** `Wunker-2016-Jobs to be done - A roadmap for customer-centered innovation.pdf`

**Use for:**

- Jobs Atlas: jobs, job drivers, current approaches/pain points, success criteria, obstacles, value, and competition;
- job drivers as attitudes, background, and circumstances;
- stakeholder analysis beyond the purchaser;
- adoption versus use obstacles;
- value capture and direct/indirect competition;
- project objectives, research planning, ideation, reframing, and experimentation;
- quick-reference summaries (PDF 163–171).

**Evidence anchors:** book map at PDF 18–25; job concepts and drivers in early Part I; current approaches and pain points around PDF 56 onward; success criteria around PDF 67 onward; Part II around PDF 115–159; appendix PDF 163–171.

### 3.8 Ulwick — *Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice*

**File:** `Ulwick-2016-Jobs to be done - Theory to practice.pdf`

**Use for:**

- six-type Jobs-to-be-Done Needs Framework (PDF 47–61);
- core functional job, solution neutrality, and job stability as formulation-specific assumptions (PDF 53–57);
- desired outcome statements and related/emotional/social/consumption-chain/financial needs (PDF 57–61);
- ODI process and growth strategy (PDF 62–123);
- job statement form, job map, and universal eight steps (around PDF 87–92);
- desired outcome grammar (around PDF 93–94);
- qualitative-to-quantitative workflow and opportunity scoring (PDF 105–112);
- case studies (PDF 124–160);
- 84-step practitioner workflow (PDF 162–174);
- glossary, including the non-negative-gap opportunity formula (PDF 186–194).

**Formula:** `opportunity = importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0)`. The narrative example around PDF 109–110 omits the explicit `max` but the glossary includes it. Use the glossary form and disclose the scale.

**Critical note:** The book reports an 86% success rate from interviews with Strategyn clients and 21 launched initiatives (PDF 21–25). Treat this as author-reported, company-associated evidence with self-selected success metrics, not an independent controlled effectiveness estimate.

**Copyright note:** The PDF copyright page states that reproduction/distribution requires permission and later page headers state distribution prohibited. Retain only paraphrased method notes in this skill; do not bundle or redistribute the source PDF.

### 3.9 Kullak, Baier, and Woratschek — “Jobs-to-be-done Theory and Application”

**File:** `Kullak-2023-Jobs-to-be-done theory and application.pdf`

**Use for:** a recent four-page synthesis of definition, functional/personal/social categories, a three-step identify-understand-provide procedure, services, needs, and consumer job journeys; use its bibliography to identify further literature (PDF 1–4).

**Do not use for:** replacing primary sources or assuming all cited extensions are included in the supplied corpus.

### 3.10 Silverstein, Samuel, and DeCarlo — *The Innovator's Toolkit*

**File:** `Silverstein-2009-The innovator's toolkit - Front matter.pdf`

**Use for:**

- locating JTBD and Outcome Expectations within a broader innovation system. The table of contents places JTBD as Technique 1, Outcome Expectations as Technique 2, Value Quotient as Technique 3, ethnography as Technique 4, and Job Scoping as Technique 7 (PDF 4–5);
- the book's Introduction (roughly PDF 16–30), which does argue substantively that innovation should start from jobs to be done and solution-neutral outcome expectations, and warns against confusing solution requirements with outcome expectations. This is citable text, not merely a contents listing.

Note that Kullak et al. (2023) cite this book at page 4 as a source for the functional/personal/social job trichotomy, and that page falls inside the supplied front matter.

**Coverage limitation:** Only front matter is supplied — the Wiley `.fmatter` extract. The numbered technique instructions (including Technique 1, Jobs To Be Done) are absent. Cite the Introduction if used; never cite the technique procedures as read.

### 3.11 Christensen — *The Innovator's Dilemma*

**File:** `Christensen-1997-The innovator's dilemma.pdf`

**Use for:** the separate disruption-theory background: technology trajectories, incumbent customer/resource dependence, and the organizational logic of failure.

**Do not use for:** the mature JTBD definition, switching forces, ODI, or job interviewing. The PDF identifies copyright years 1997 and 2000; verify which edition the bibliography requires.

### 3.12 Christensen, Grossman, and Hwang — *The Innovator's Prescription*

**File:** `Christensen-2009-The innovator's prescription.pdf`

**Use for:**

- introductory job explanation in healthcare (PDF 55–60);
- organizational models focused on distinct health-service jobs (PDF 109–116);
- adherence and financial-health examples (PDF 191–194);
- multiple jobs among patients, providers, employers, payers, and policy actors (PDF 247 onward as relevant);
- the principle that quality and integration should be considered relative to a job.

**Caution:** Treat examples as healthcare-system theorizing and case illustration, not clinical-effectiveness evidence or a universal stakeholder map.

### 3.13 Blank and Engel — NSF I-Corps Teaching Handbook

**File:** `Blank-2016-I-Corps teaching handbook.pdf`

**Use for:** experiential learning, search versus execution, business-model hypotheses, customer discovery, in-person/open-ended interviewing, MVP discussion, and weekly hypothesis–experiment–result–iteration records. The course description and detailed assignments are especially visible at PDF 92–108.

**Caution:** This January 2016 handbook is a teaching draft, depends on external course materials, and is not a JTBD method.

### 3.14 Haddad and colleagues — mHealth adaptation study

**File:** `Haddad-2022-Using I-Corps methods to adapt a mHealth obesity treatment.pdf`

**Use for:**

- a peer-reviewed empirical example combining stakeholder hypotheses, jobs/pains/value language, and I-Corps customer discovery;
- six stakeholder groups nested within three categories: service consumers (outpatients/CMHC and Clubhouse members, n=4); clinician consumers (nurses n=4; PSR counsellors, community support workers, case managers, social workers n=5; prescribing clinicians n=8); and decision makers (local clinic and clubhouse administrators n=5; state administrators n=3);
- 29 recorded, transcribed interviews in total, with coding continued to thematic saturation using pragmatic-variant grounded theory (PDF 1–3);
- a four-step description of I-Corps methodology (PDF 2);
- cross-role shared and differing themes used to adapt intervention content, training, and delivery;
- transparent strengths and limitations (PDF 9–10).

**Caution:** Do not copy its stakeholder categories or four themes into a new study as predetermined truth. The article reports one intervention and context.

## 4. Corpus-level cautions

### 4.1 Evidence balance

The corpus is rich in conceptual and practitioner material but limited in independent peer-reviewed tests of JTBD mechanisms. Search beyond this corpus when the thesis requires a systematic review, current evidence base, scale validation, or comparison with alternative theories.

### 4.2 Formulation differences

- Christensen centers progress in circumstance and switching causality.
- Ulwick centers a stable functional job and outcome metrics.
- Wunker provides a broad Jobs Atlas and innovation roadmap.
- Anthony offers opportunity-discovery questions and methods.
- I-Corps structures hypothesis-driven customer discovery but is not JTBD.

Do not construct a synthetic “canonical JTBD” without attribution.

### 4.3 Tensions worth reporting

- Christensen warns that customers may not articulate requirements fully; Bettencourt/Ulwick argue that customers can articulate solution-neutral outcomes when asked correctly.
- Christensen makes circumstance central to defining a job; Ulwick emphasizes the core functional job's stability and uses context as a clarifier or segmentation factor.
- Christensen emphasizes depth of narrative and forces; ODI requires standardized outcomes and quantitative prioritization.
- Wunker treats attitudes/background/circumstances as job drivers; this is not identical to either model above.

Use these tensions to justify methodological choices rather than erasing them.

### 4.4 Duplicate and overlapping evidence

The 2016 HBR article and *Competing Against Luck* substantially overlap. The two 2007 management articles and the 2008 chapter also repeat examples and methods. Avoid counting repeated milkshake or QuickBooks examples as multiple independent empirical confirmations.

### 4.5 Source access and copyright

Several PDFs contain individual-use or distribution restrictions. This skill includes only original synthesis, short formulas, bibliographic data, and method descriptions. Never copy or package the PDFs or reproduce long passages.
