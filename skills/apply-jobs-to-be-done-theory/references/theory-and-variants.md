## 1. Shared proposition

Use the following as the narrow shared core:

- Actors use, combine, avoid, delay, or replace solutions while trying to accomplish something or make progress.
- The relevant unit of analysis is the job, not the person or the product category.
- Products, services, people, routines, workarounds, and non-action can compete when they address the same job.
- Functional performance alone may not explain choice; emotional, social, institutional, and situational conditions may matter.
- Innovation opportunities become visible when current ways of getting a job done are inadequate, costly, risky, inaccessible, or resisted.

**Circumstance is not part of the shared core.** How much context belongs inside the job is precisely where the formulations diverge: Christensen makes circumstance constitutive of the job, Ulwick treats the core functional job as context-stable and admits context only as a clarifier or segmentation factor, and Wunker externalizes it as job drivers that shift job priority. Take a position on this and state it; do not assert "the job in its context" as though all formulations agreed.

Do not present more specific propositions as universally shared. The supplied corpus contains multiple practitioner traditions with different definitions, units, and procedures.

## 2. The major formulations

### 2.1 Christensen: progress in a circumstance

Define a job as the progress an individual seeks in a particular circumstance. Treat circumstance as intrinsic rather than a demographic modifier. Explain choice by reconstructing the causal story through which an actor pulls a solution into life.

Analyze:

- the moment or period of struggle;
- the desired progress;
- functional, emotional, and social dimensions;
- what must be fired for a new solution to be hired;
- the push of the current situation;
- the pull of the new solution;
- habit or inertia favoring the present;
- anxiety or uncertainty opposing change;
- Big Hire (purchase/adoption) versus Little Hire (actual/repeated use);
- the experiences required to resolve the job, summarized as a job spec (defined with a template in section 5.5; note it is an innovator-side design artifact, not a finding).

Use this formulation for retrospective switching narratives, adoption, persistence, abandonment, and experience design. Treat causal language carefully in academic research: interviews can support a mechanism-oriented interpretation but do not alone establish population-level causal effects.

### 2.2 Ulwick: functional job and desired outcomes

Define the core functional job as the solution-neutral task or goal executed by the end user. Treat it as stable enough to anchor market analysis. Map the job as a process, then capture the criteria used to judge success at every step as desired outcome statements.

Differentiate six input types:

1. Core functional job.
2. Desired outcomes tied to the core job.
3. Related functional jobs.
4. Emotional and social jobs.
5. Consumption-chain jobs across purchase, receipt, installation, setup, learning, interface, transport, cleaning, storage, maintenance, upgrade, repair, and disposal.
6. Financial outcomes used by the buyer.

These six inputs are not all held by the same person. Ulwick distinguishes three customer types, and each supplies different inputs:

| Customer type | Supplies | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| End user (functional job executor) | Desired outcomes on the core functional job; emotional, social, and related jobs | The anchor for the core functional job |
| Product lifecycle support team | Desired outcomes on consumption-chain jobs | Installs, sets up, transports, maintains, repairs, cleans, upgrades, disposes; may overlap with the end user |
| Purchase decision maker (buyer) | Financial desired outcomes | Frequently a different person from the end user |

Sample and interview these roles separately. Attributing consumption-chain or financial outcomes to the end user misassigns the evidence.

Use the universal job-map steps as a prompt, not a finding: define, locate, prepare, confirm, execute, monitor, modify, and conclude. A specific job may not require every step.

Use ODI when the study must convert qualitative learning into standardized outcomes and quantitative importance/satisfaction analysis. Do not treat ODI's commercial success claims or score thresholds as independent academic validation. Outcome-Driven Innovation and ODI are associated with Strategyn; retain attribution and do not imply an official certification.

### 2.3 Wunker: Jobs Atlas and Jobs Roadmap

Construct a Jobs Atlas with:

- jobs to be done;
- job drivers: attitudes, background, and circumstances affecting priority;
- current approaches and pain points;
- success criteria;
- adoption and use obstacles;
- value for customers, stakeholders, and provider organizations;
- direct, indirect, workaround, and nonconsumption competition.

Use the broader Jobs Roadmap to move from objectives and research planning through ideation, reframing, experimentation, and iteration. Use this formulation for applied innovation portfolios or public-sector work that requires both customer insight and implementation strategy.

Do not equate Wunker's “job drivers” with Christensen's full definition of circumstance. Drivers explain variation in job priority; in Christensen's account circumstance is part of the job's causal definition.

### 2.4 Anthony and colleagues: opportunity discovery

Frame a job as the fundamental problem a customer faces in a given circumstance. Investigate five questions:

1. What fundamental problem is the customer trying to solve?
2. What objectives are used to evaluate solutions?
3. What barriers constrain the solution?
4. What solutions are available, including compensating behaviors?
5. What opportunities exist to improve the quality of getting the job done?

Use brainstorming, light customer interaction, in-depth interviews, observation, diaries, compensating-behavior analysis, and customer case research as complementary clue sources. Prioritize early opportunities using importance, frequency, and frustration only as an exploratory screen. Do not confuse that scoring method with ODI's opportunity algorithm.

### 2.5 I-Corps-adjacent customer discovery

Treat I-Corps as an iterative hypothesis-testing and commercialization/implementation pedagogy, not a JTBD theory variant. It can complement JTBD by:

- identifying stakeholder groups;
- stating testable problem and value-proposition hypotheses;
- conducting repeated interviews;
- recording what was expected, done, found, and changed;
- iterating or pivoting an offering.

Use JTBD to improve what is investigated in stakeholder interviews. Use I-Corps to structure learning cycles. Do not infer that every “pain,” “gain,” or value-proposition item is a job.

### 2.6 Disruptive innovation as adjacent theory

Treat disruption theory as a separate account of how entrants, technologies, business models, and incumbent resource-allocation processes evolve. JTBD may help define the customer progress or nonconsumption an entrant addresses, but it does not by itself establish that an innovation is disruptive.

Require evidence about the disruption mechanism before applying the label: initial foothold, performance trajectory, business model, incumbent response, and movement into the mainstream. Do not cite *The Innovator's Dilemma* as the source of the mature JTBD construct.

## 3. Construct distinctions

| Construct | Meaning | Diagnostic question | Frequent error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job / desired progress | What the actor is trying to accomplish or become able to do | What change is sought in this episode? | Writing a product, feature, or activity |
| Circumstance | Conditions shaping what progress and trade-offs matter | What was happening, when, where, with whom, and under which constraints? | Treating age or persona as sufficient |
| Need | **Formulation-dependent; see the note below** | Which formulation's definition of "need" is in force here? | Using one generic sense of "need" across formulations |
| Desired outcome / success criterion | Standard for judging how well a job is performed | How would the actor know the job was done well? | Writing a solution or vague adjective |
| Solution | Anything hired, combined, tolerated, delayed, or fired | What was used instead, including doing nothing? | Restricting competition to a product category |
| Feature | Attribute of a proposed or existing solution | What does the offering contain or enable? | Embedding the feature in the job statement |
| Activity | Observable action | What did the actor do? | Assuming the action reveals the underlying job |
| Pain point | Friction in a current approach | Where did the existing approach fail or cost too much? | Treating pain alone as the desired progress |
| Journey stage | Position in a temporal/service sequence | At what point did the interaction occur? | Treating journey maps as job maps |
| Stakeholder job | A particular actor's sought progress | Whose job is this? | Collapsing user, buyer, provider, and regulator |
| Motivation/value | More abstract preference or principle | Why does this matter? | Choosing an abstraction too broad to guide analysis |

### 3.1 "Need" is the most contested term in the corpus

The supplied sources do not share one definition. Do not import a generic sense of "need" and do not let it drift within one study:

| Source | Definition in force | Consequence for coding |
|---|---|---|
| Ulwick (*Theory to Practice* glossary) | A need **is** a metric used to measure successful execution of a functional or consumption-chain job; explicitly synonymous with *desired outcome* | Every "need" must be written in outcome grammar and be ratable for importance/satisfaction |
| Bettencourt ("Debunking Myths") | A need is a solution-neutral requirement statement that customers can articulate when asked correctly; the enemy is solution-tainted wording, not need language itself | Needs are elicitable but must be scrubbed of solutions |
| Christensen (*Competing Against Luck*) | Needs are treated as broader and vaguer than jobs; a job carries circumstance and is not reducible to a stated need | "Need" is not an analytic code; use progress, circumstance, and dimensions instead |

State in the methods text which definition the study adopts. If the study uses Ulwick's, then "need" and "desired outcome" are one construct and must not also appear as a separate broader category. If the study uses Christensen's, do not code `NEED` at all.

## 4. Integrative model

Use this layered model only as a transparent synthesis:

1. **System and roles:** Identify executor, buyer, user, gatekeeper, implementer, supporter, and affected party.
2. **Episode and circumstance:** Locate the concrete temporal, social, material, institutional, and life-stage setting.
3. **Desired progress/core job:** State what the focal actor is trying to accomplish without naming a solution.
4. **Dimensions and related jobs:** Separate functional, emotional, social, related, consumption-chain, and financial concerns.
5. **Current approaches:** Document sequences, alternatives, workarounds, nonconsumption, and pain points.
6. **Choice dynamics:** Reconstruct triggers, push, pull, habit/inertia, anxiety, adoption, use, and abandonment.
7. **Evaluation:** Identify success criteria or desired outcomes and the trade-offs among them.
8. **Consequences:** Examine individual, stakeholder, organizational, and system outcomes.

State which layers come from which formulation. Do not present the synthesis as a single canonical JTBD model.

## 5. Job-statement rules

### 5.1 Circumstance-plus-progress statement (Christensen-compatible)

Use a circumstance-plus-progress form:

> When [specific circumstance/struggle], I want to [make this progress], so I can [meaningful consequence if supported by data].

**Attribution warning.** This template is a practitioner convention (commonly called a "job story") and **does not appear in the supplied corpus**. *Competing Against Luck* and the 2016 HBR article describe progress, circumstance, dimensions, forces, and the job spec, but give no such fill-in-the-blank format. Cite it as an analytic device the study adopted, not as Christensen's formulation, and do not attach a page number from the corpus to it. If a thesis requires a sourced format, either cite the job-story literature directly from outside this corpus or state that the wording is the researcher's own operationalization.

Treat “so I can” as optional. Avoid forcing a ladder to an abstract life value. A useful statement must retain enough context to explain why alternatives and trade-offs change.

### 5.2 Ulwick-oriented statement

Use:

> [verb] + [object of the verb] + [contextual clarifier]

Example: “coordinate follow-up care after discharge” is solution-neutral; “use the hospital app after discharge” is not.

Check that the statement:

- begins with an action verb;
- names the object or goal;
- includes only necessary context;
- avoids products, brands, technologies, and proposed features;
- sits at a useful level of abstraction;
- can anchor multiple existing or future solutions.

### 5.3 Desired outcome statement

Use:

> [direction of improvement] + [performance metric] + [object of control] + [contextual clarifier]

Examples of directions include minimize, increase, reduce, ensure, and maximize. Prefer observable or judgeable metrics such as time, likelihood, frequency, effort, variability, accuracy, or waste. Do not use an adjective such as “easy” without defining what easier means.

### 5.4 Candidate status

This is statement maturity, a different axis from the three-tier analytic confidence in `analysis-and-codebook.md` section 5.2. Keep them in separate columns; "provisional" here means "derived from one case so far", not "low confidence".

Label statements as:

- provisional after one case;
- recurring after multiple independent episodes;
- refined after negative-case analysis;
- survey-ready only after wording review and cognitive testing;
- final only relative to a specified dataset and research purpose.

### 5.5 Job spec

The formulation table lists the job spec as a typical Christensen-route output, so define it rather than leaving it as a loose phrase.

**Definition (from the supplied source, PDF 84–100).** The job itself frames the circumstance *from the consumer's point of view* as they struggle to make progress. The job spec is the counterpart *from the innovator's point of view*: what must be designed, developed, and delivered so that an offering resolves the job well. It captures four things:

1. the functional, emotional, and social dimensions that define the desired progress;
2. the trade-offs the actor is willing to make;
3. the full set of competing solutions that must be beaten;
4. the obstacles and anxieties that must be overcome.

It then becomes the blueprint for the experiences of purchase and use that the offering must create.

**Academic consequence.** A job spec is a design artifact, not an empirical finding. In a thesis it belongs in the implications or design section, never in findings, and every line must be traceable to an evidenced row above it. Writing one does not require that the study also evaluate the resulting design.

| Job spec field | Content | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Focal job and circumstance | The selected job statement and its scope conditions | Episode IDs |
| Functional dimension | What must be accomplished | `FUNCTIONAL_DIMENSION` codes |
| Emotional dimension | What must be felt or avoided | `EMOTIONAL_DIMENSION` codes |
| Social dimension | How the actor must be seen or related to | `SOCIAL_DIMENSION` codes |
| Accepted trade-offs | What the actor will give up, and what they will not | `TRADE_OFF` codes |
| Competing solutions to beat | Every alternative including workaround and non-action | `CURRENT_SOLUTION`, `ALTERNATIVE`, `COMPENSATING_BEHAVIOR`, `NONCONSUMPTION` |
| Obstacles and anxieties to overcome | Barriers separated into pre-adoption and in-use | `ADOPTION_OBSTACLE`, `USE_OBSTACLE`, `ANXIETY_UNCERTAINTY` |
| Required experiences | Purchase/adoption and use experiences the offering must deliver | Inference, labelled as such |
| Unresolved / not evidenced | Fields the data could not populate | State explicitly rather than filling the gap |

Leave the last row visible. A job spec with no blank cells after a small qualitative study is almost certainly part invention.

## 6. Scope and boundary conditions

Document:

- whether the job is episodic, recurrent, or longitudinal;
- whether the focal choice is individual, household, professional, organizational, or policy-mediated;
- whether the executor can choose freely or acts under institutional constraints;
- whether reported progress is aspirational, attempted, achieved, or retrospectively rationalized;
- whether multiple actors must coordinate to complete the job;
- whether the analysis covers adoption, actual use, outcomes, or all three;
- whether the market/setting has changed since the episode;
- whether cultural and linguistic meanings alter the job or only its expression.

Avoid claiming that jobs are universally stable across time and geography unless the study actually supports that proposition. Treat stability as a formulation-specific assumption to examine.

## 7. Common category errors

Reject or repair:

- “The job is to use our platform.” This is a solution.
- “The job is convenience.” This is a vague criterion.
- “The job is health.” This is too broad.
- “The job is to click submit.” This is an interface activity unless the study is specifically about that action.
- “Millennials have this job.” This substitutes a demographic segment for a circumstance.
- “The customer journey proves the job.” A journey describes interactions; the job explains desired progress.
- “The highest ODI score proves causality.” A score prioritizes rated outcomes under a stated design.
- “This startup is disruptive because it serves a job.” JTBD fit is not evidence of disruption.
- “Interviewees validated the job statement, so it is objective.” Participant feedback is one input, not a substitute for analysis.
- “No new code appeared, therefore saturation was reached.” Define the type and scope of adequacy claimed.
