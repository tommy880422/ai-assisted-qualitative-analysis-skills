# Transcription and Anonymization

The recording is the original data; a transcript is a selective representation of it, and analysis already begins during transcription (p. 166). When transcripts are the material to be coded, declare the transcription rules before transcribing and apply the same rules to every recording. Decide the rules before transcription starts, and disclose them in the report.

## Procedure From Recording to Software

1. Determine a rule set, or adopt an established transcription system suited to the planned analysis.
2. Transcribe the material, or the relevant parts of it, manually or with an automatic transcription tool.
3. Proof-read, edit, and correct the transcripts against the recording.
4. Anonymize or pseudonymize.
5. Format so the QDA program can process speakers, paragraphs, and timestamps.
6. Save and archive in a stable document format.
7. Import into the software.

## Choose the Level of Accuracy

Accuracy is an analytic decision, not only a budget decision. Even simple transcription takes roughly five times the length of the recording; capturing intonation, dialect, and overlapping speech in group settings costs considerably more. Transcribing too precisely can also obstruct analysis when dialect or paralinguistic detail makes the text hard to read. A relatively simple system is sufficient for most qualitative content analysis. Reserve complex systems such as GAT, HIAT, or CHAT for linguistic and conversation-analytic work.

## A Sufficient Rule Set

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1 | One conversational contribution per paragraph, including short interjections such as “yes” or “exactly”; separate speaker changes with a blank line |
| 2 | Prefix interviewer or moderator paragraphs with `I:` or `M:` and respondents with a unique abbreviation; number them (`M1:`, `R2:`) when several people are recorded; set speaker identifiers in bold |
| 3 | Transcribe verbatim rather than phonetically or as summary; render dialect into the standard language |
| 4 | Smooth language and punctuation slightly toward written standards |
| 5 | Mark long, clear pauses as `(…)` |
| 6 | Underline emphasized terms |
| 7 | Capitalize anything said loudly |
| 8 | Omit interviewer backchannels (“mhm”, “aha”) unless they interrupt the speaker or function as an answer |
| 9 | Transcribe fillers such as “um” only when they carry meaning |
| 10 | Note disruptions in double parentheses with the cause, e.g. `((telephone rings))` |
| 11 | Note supporting vocalizations in single parentheses, e.g. `(laughs)`, `(sighs)` |
| 12 | Note non-verbal activity from video in single parentheses, e.g. `(turns away)` |
| 13 | Mark unintelligible words as `(unclear)`; bracket suspected wording with a question mark, e.g. `(kobold?)` |
| 14 | Insert timestamps at the end of each contribution, and within long paragraphs at incomprehensible passages |

Automatic transcription reliably delivers only rules 1 and 2, partially delivers 3, 4, 13, and 14, and does not deliver the emphasis, volume, backchannel, filler, disruption, vocalization, and non-verbal rules. Anything the tool cannot produce must be added by a human listener or dropped from the analysis by explicit decision.

## Anonymization

Anonymize after transcription is complete when the material contains many identifying details; anonymizing during transcription overloads transcribers, especially external ones.

- Replace names, places, dates, and comparable details with pseudonyms or abbreviations.
- Choose pseudonyms that preserve the characteristics that matter analytically, such as cultural origin, gender, and age cohort. Do not rename a Japanese exchange student “Horst”, and do not use frivolous names.
- Generalize places and dates instead of substituting equally specific ones: “small town”, “village”, “last winter”.
- Keep a decryption table recording every change, stored separately from the data and under the applicable data-protection rules.
- Save the finished file in a stable format and back it up to a second location; a copy on the same disk is not a backup.

## Report

State the transcription rules used, who transcribed and whether software was involved, what proof-reading was done, how anonymization was handled, and whether the recording remains available for re-checking. Transcription decisions bound what the analysis can later claim about emphasis, interaction, and silence.
