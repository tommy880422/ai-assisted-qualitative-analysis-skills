# Select the Type of Qualitative Content Analysis

## Common Foundation

All three types:

- begin with research questions and contextual reading;
- use categories as systematic analytic devices;
- combine interpretation with transparent procedure;
- preserve cases as well as coded segments;
- write memos and case summaries;
- continue analysis after coding;
- may use qualitative and descriptive quantitative information when appropriate.

## Structuring QCA

Choose when the goal is to organize material by substantive topics or dimensions and compare cases or groups.

Typical products:

- main and subcategory system;
- coded corpus;
- thematic case summaries;
- cases-by-categories matrix;
- within-category and cross-category analysis;
- comparisons by relevant attributes.

## Evaluative QCA

Choose when the goal is to assess cases on clearly defined evaluative dimensions such as degree, quality, orientation, or level.

Typical products:

- thematic and evaluative categories;
- ordinal or dichotomous evaluative characteristics, including “unable to classify” (pp. 36, 127);
- case profiles;
- quote matrices and cross-tabs;
- comparison of evaluation with context or attributes.

Do not hide normative criteria. Define what each level means and what evidence supports it.

## Type-Building QCA

Choose when the goal is to develop an empirically grounded typology based on meaningful combinations of attributes or category configurations.

Typical products:

- selected type-building dimensions;
- case profiles or attribute space;
- grouped configurations;
- internally coherent and externally distinct types;
- type descriptions, prototypes, and deviant cases.

## Decision Table

| Main question | Select |
|---|---|
| What topics or dimensions appear, and how do cases compare? | Structuring QCA |
| How should cases be assessed along explicit dimensions? | Evaluative QCA |
| What recurring configurations form meaningful case types? | Type-building QCA |

A project may sequence types — type-building usually builds on a structuring or evaluative analysis (p. 80) — but state which method answers which question. The decision table is this skill's summary, not a table from the book.
